Isn’t Segregation Illegal?

On wet waste, dry waste, and the kind of segregation nobody should be against

Paa Sankar June 2026 12 min read

Isn’t segregation illegal?

Yes. When it’s about people — race, religion, caste, where someone’s grandparents happened to come from — every constitution worth having outlaws it, and rightly so.

We are going to talk about a different kind of segregation. The kind municipal corporations across India have been asking for, year after year. The kind most of us still don’t do. And the kind that — unlike the other one — actually needs to happen, every single day, in every single home. Source segregation. Of garbage.

Walk into any conversation about Indian cities and waste, and you’ll hear some version of the same split. A handful of cities — Indore chief among them — have built their civic identity around getting this right: door-to-door collection, daily segregation, composting at scale, repeated national recognition for it. Most others have tried the identical rulebook and watched it fail. Same coloured bins. Same campaigns. Same fines, even. Different result.

What, then, is the difference?

The cities that get it right spend the first year explaining why.

Indore’s municipal corporation didn’t just hand out coloured bins and a deadline — it sent NGO volunteers door-to-door for months to explain, household by household, what happens when wet and dry waste meet. It commissioned a popular playback singer to record a civic anthem that garbage vans across the city still play each morning as they make their rounds. It pulled schoolchildren into the campaign too, on the theory that a child who understands why will go home and explain it to a parent who won’t listen to anyone else. None of this taught a single resident the chemistry of composting. What it did was make the why impossible to avoid — long before the bylaw and its penalty ever got enforced. Here’s more on how Indore actually built this habit, not just the rule.

Most city corporations have already reached for the obvious lever: mandatory source segregation, backed by a penalty for not doing it. On paper, this rule exists in a long list of Indian cities today. In practice, it’s observed more in the breach, and the reason is almost always the same — penalty changes behaviour only as long as someone is watching for it.

Think about the helmet rule. Most of us wear one not because anyone ever explained what’s actually at stake — our own skull — but because a traffic policeman might stop us and write a ticket if we don’t. The fear of the fine is doing all the work, not an understanding of the risk. Take the policeman off that stretch of road for a week, and watch how many helmets quietly disappear along with him. Source segregation rules are following the exact same script. The penalty exists. The behaviour mostly doesn’t — and won’t, until the why comes first and the fine becomes a backstop nobody actually needs, rather than the whole reason anyone bothers.

We are also used to someone else cleaning up after us. It’s a fairly comfortable way to live, in a country with no shortage of hands willing to do that work for a price most of us can afford. The street sweeper takes the litter. The garbage truck takes the bin. The waste picker takes whatever’s left and somehow makes a living sorting through it.

That entire chain works — right up until the very first link. The chain breaks the moment you, inside your own kitchen, mix the wet and the dry. From that point on, it’s not somebody else’s problem anymore — it’s ours, all the way down the line, because there is no un-mixing it at any later stage of the process.

Here’s the part nobody explains clearly enough: it isn’t the waste that’s the problem. It’s the mixing.

Used newspaper sitting next to an old shirt? Pick either one up next week, next month, doesn’t matter — still newspaper, still a shirt. Easy to separate, any time you like. But drop a fistful of tea leaves or half a meal on top of a plastic wrapper, and within a day you no longer have wrapper-plus-food. You have one wet, contaminated mess that nobody downstream is going to sort through by hand. The mixing did the damage. Not the waste itself.

If you’ve ever made idli batter, you already understand this instinctively. Rice gets soaked separately. Urad dal gets soaked separately. Each needs its own soaking time, its own grinding time. You only combine them at the very last step.

Once they’re mixed, you can’t go back and pull the rice out of the dal again. Garbage works exactly the same way.

And here’s what makes the mixing such a waste, in the most literal sense. Roughly 70% of what an average Indian household throws out, by weight, is wet waste — vegetable peels, food scraps, tea leaves, the everyday leftovers of cooking. A recent door-to-door waste audit in an Indian city found almost exactly this split, if you want to read about it — and nature has been processing exactly this kind of waste for longer than cities have existed. Left alone, in its own bin, it composts. It becomes soil. It costs nothing and asks nothing more complicated of you than a lid and a daily habit.

Mixed with a plastic wrapper, that same 70% becomes something else entirely. It becomes the reason a half-eaten sandwich tossed onto a heap of plastic bags attracts a stray dog or a cow — and the animal doesn’t carefully eat around the plastic. It eats the food and the wrapper together, because it has no way of telling the two apart once we’ve stopped bothering to. Veterinary hospitals in India now treat this often enough that it barely makes news — one government hospital in Odisha alone pulled close to 50 kilograms of plastic out of a single stray cow’s stomach after a four-hour surgery, and it wasn’t even the first time that hospital had done it. Seventy percent of your bin could have quietly become fertiliser. Mixed in with the rest, it becomes an animal’s emergency surgery instead.

The Four Bins

Once you understand why, the how barely needs explaining. It comes down to four categories, none requiring a special degree:

  • Wet waste — cooking waste, vegetable and fruit cuttings, leftover or stale food. A separate bin in the kitchen, emptied daily. Wet waste sitting for a week isn’t segregation — it’s an accidental compost experiment.
  • Dry waste — old clothes, plastic wrappers, milk packets (rinsed, please), used medicine strips, batteries, e-waste. Once a week is enough; nothing in here is racing to rot.
  • Medical waste — blood-tinged cotton, sanitary pads, anything that’s touched a body in a way that matters. Wrap it in newspaper, mark it with a red sticker, hand it to whoever your municipality has designated. Most already have a system for this, even if nobody’s told you.
  • Hazardous waste — broken glass, leaking batteries, anything genuinely dangerous to handle bare-handed. Never mix this in with the rest. Pack it separately, however you can, and hand it over on its own.

The last one deserves a quick reasoning check — more than the others. Somewhere downstream, a real person is going to sort through your dry waste bag with their bare hands — separating newspaper from plastic wrappers — and they won’t be expecting broken glass or a leaking battery to be sitting in there with everything else. They are humans too. The five extra seconds it takes you to keep that separate is the difference between their evening ending normally and their evening ending in an emergency room.

In our own home, we’ve been doing this for years now — long enough that it’s stopped feeling like an extra chore and started feeling like just another part of how a kitchen runs. A “smart” cup on the counter holds the day’s wet waste, and once or twice a day it gets emptied into a composting bin lined with coco peat — to soak up the moisture — and a culture of microorganisms that hurries the whole process along. The bin does the actual work, quietly turning everything into something we can use in our own plants within weeks.

It’s a standing joke in our house at this point: the bin is smarter than the humans living with it. It never argues, never postpones, never says it’ll get to it tomorrow. It just sits there, composting, while the rest of us talk endlessly about discipline and somehow still manage to leave the dishes in the sink.

Get the wet waste right — separated out and gone before it can mix with anything else — and you’ve already done more than two-thirds of the work, since that’s roughly how much of your bin it accounts for by weight. That alone makes you something close to a first-class citizen of your own street. And it does something else, too: dry waste that’s never been allowed to touch wet waste in the first place turns into a tame beast. Let’s see why.

Dry Waste Is a Treasure, Not Trash

Dry waste is also where segregation quietly pays for itself. Almost everything in that bin — cloth, plastic, paper, metal, e-waste — has commercial value to somebody. Recyclers want it. Re-users want it even more.

In our own apartment, this has played out in a way I didn’t expect. Maintenance staff, domestic help, and security personnel — the people who keep the building running every single day — are the ones who walk into our designated dry-waste area most often, to see what’s been set aside that week. Old clothes nobody’s wearing anymore. A bag that’s still perfectly usable. Nobody hands anything to anybody directly, and that’s precisely what makes it work. No awkward moment of charity, no need to ask, no one keeping count of who took what. You set something aside because you no longer need it. Someone else picks it up because they do. Both sides keep their dignity, simply because the system never required either of you to acknowledge the other.

This is what understanding the why actually produces on the ground — not a stricter rule, but a small, working economy nobody had to design on a whiteboard.

One Ask, If You’re a Do-er

The world divides fairly cleanly into people who do, and people who don’t. If none of this has moved you, that’s alright — though we’d genuinely like to hear what would, because the people most worth convincing are rarely the ones already convinced.

There’s also a reason we keep pushing the idea of staying in touch as a community of do-ers, rather than just handing you a how-to and wishing you luck. There’s no shortage of material online telling you how to segregate. What’s much harder to find is an answer to the specific, slightly absurd question you’ll inevitably hit once you actually start — what do I do with a single broken umbrella, where does a used inhaler go, how do I get a building’s security guard on board when the residents’ association won’t budge. Generic guides don’t cover that. Someone who’s already solved that exact problem, in a kitchen or a building much like yours, usually has — and that’s worth more than another article.

Years ago, when I still read Reader’s Digest — yes, I know, this was a long time before I graduated to reading nonsense instead — one short piece stayed with me longer than anything else from those pages. A man wanted to change his country. Years passed, and he couldn’t. He scaled it down to his county instead, and the county wouldn’t budge for him either. Eventually he gave up on scale altogether and decided he’d simply change himself. Nothing more ambitious than that.

He did. And the people who knew him began changing too, the way habits move between people who actually live near each other. It spread to his street, then his town, then quietly past the county line — and years later, what he could never force from the top had moved, on its own, from the bottom.

That’s the size of spark we’re actually asking for here. Not a city-wide rollout, not a corporation announcement, not even your whole building on day one. Just you, changing one habit at your own kitchen counter, starting today. Enough drops moving the same direction eventually make a wave — whether or not you’re ever around to see how far it travelled.

So if you’re a do-er, or you want to be one and just haven’t started: take the pledge. Tell us what you already do, or what you’ll start doing in the next fifteen days. That’s it. No apartment committee to convince first, no perfect setup required on day one — just an honest answer about where you stand today, and where you’re headed next.

We’ll do the rest. We’ll help you figure out what’s realistically possible in your kitchen, your building, your street. And if enough of us in a neighbourhood do this together, fifty households’ worth of dry waste stops being garbage — and starts being a resource somebody is genuinely glad to come and collect.

We can wait for the corporation to clean up what we wouldn’t sort ourselves — and pay for that convenience, one way or another. Or we can solve it at the SOURCE, in our own kitchen, on our own street, starting today — pull out the root, and every symptom downstream disappears like thin air.
