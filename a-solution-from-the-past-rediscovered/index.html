A solution from the past, rediscovered

If human nature doesn't change, why would AI?

Paa Sankar September 2026 6 min read

Ask an AI chatbot right now to explain curiosity, and it will hand you a complete, fluent, fully resolved answer in about four seconds. Which is, if you think about it for a moment, close to the least curious thing you could possibly do with the question. Last time, we established that this is the actual cost of instant AI answers, they close the exact gap that was supposed to trigger learning, before the learner has time to feel it as a gap at all.

What we didn't get into is that this isn't a new discovery about human psychology. It's a very old one, and it comes with something more useful than a diagnosis, it comes with a working demonstration. Now that we know the problem, let's see if there's a solution.

The word that beat the theory by two thousand years

George Loewenstein's 1994 paper on curiosity is the standard modern reference. His argument: curiosity is what you feel when you notice a gap between what you know and what you want to know, and that felt gap, not raw ignorance, is the actual engine of learning.

Sanskrit philosophy got there first, and got there more precisely, by building the observation directly into a word. The Brahma Sutras, one of the foundational texts of Vedantic philosophy, open with the line athato brahma jijnasa, "now, therefore, the inquiry into Brahman." Jijnasa isn't a noun for inquiry or knowledge. Grammatically, it's a desiderative, a specific verb form built from the root "to know" that means, precisely, the desire to know. Curious? Read further here. Shankara's commentary is explicit that this desire has to exist before the actual investigating starts, it isn't the same thing as the investigation itself. That's Loewenstein's exact distinction, felt gap versus mere information, except it's sitting inside a single word rather than requiring a whole paper to establish, and it was doing that job when the Roman Republic hadn't been founded yet.

The boy who wouldn't take the bribe

If jijnasa gives you the theory, the Katha Upanishad gives you the demonstration, and it's a genuinely strange, sharp little story worth knowing on its own terms.

A boy named Nachiketa ends up, through a chain of events involving his father's careless words, standing before Yama, Death himself, and is granted three wishes. He spends the first two on ordinary things. The third is a real question: when a person dies, does anything of them persist, or not.

Yama does not want to answer this. He offers Nachiketa a way out, wealth, long life, cattle, elephants, gold, entire kingdoms, every pleasure available to a mortal, if the boy will simply withdraw the question. Nachiketa refuses every single offer. He wants the answer, not a comfortable substitute for it.

Death, the single most authoritative and terrifying source of information in the room, is trying to close the gap early, buy the question off before it gets answered properly.

Nachiketa's whole heroism, in a text that predates the Buddha, is refusing to let a difficult question resolve too easily. That's not a moral about bravery. It's a precise dramatisation of exactly what an AI chatbot does to your kid's homework question every single time, except the AI, unlike Yama, doesn't even bother offering the bribe. It just answers, and the gap closes before anyone had the chance to defend it.

Two thousand years, and the diagnosis still fits

There's a reason this matters beyond being a nice historical footnote. If a two-thousand-year-old text about a boy and Death captures something true about human psychology, that's evidence, not just decoration, that curiosity's actual shape hasn't shifted, only the tools available to interfere with it have.

The Thirukkural, written centuries later and completely independent of the Vedic tradition, makes the same point in its chapter on learning, kalvi, one that scholars regularly point to as reading like it was written last year rather than roughly 1,500 years ago. It doesn't hedge on the transformation claim either. Kural 393 puts it plainly:

கண்ணுடையர் என்பவர் கற்றோர் முகத்திரண்டு
புண்ணுடையர் கல்லாதவர்

Which comes to roughly, "the learned alone are said to truly have eyes; the unlearned carry, in their place, a wound." Not "the unlearned know less." A missing sense, replaced by an injury. That's a claim about transformation, not information, exactly the distinction this piece has been building toward. Curious? Read further here.

Kural 399 closes the loop back to curiosity directly:

தாமின் புறுவது உலகின் புறக்கண்டு
காமுறுவர் கற்றறிந் தார்

Seeing that what delights them delights the world too, the learned grow only more eager to learn. Not a finish line. A gap that gets more interesting the more of it you close, which is Loewenstein's whole theory, sitting in a couplet a millennium and a half old.

A claim about human nature that survives fifteen centuries of translation, empire, and total technological upheaval and still lands as accurate is a much stronger kind of evidence than a claim that merely sounds wise.

So what do you actually do with this

Here's the practical version, and it's the same one Yama accidentally demonstrated by trying and failing to close Nachiketa's gap. Don't resolve the question completely. Loewenstein's own advice for teachers is almost identical to Yama's failed strategy, run in reverse: give a partial, intriguing, deliberately incomplete answer, prime the gap rather than close it, because curiosity, oddly, increases with partial knowledge rather than total ignorance. Curious? Read further here. You cannot be curious about something you have zero awareness of at all. You are most curious about something you've just been shown the edge of.

An AI tutor built on this principle would behave almost nothing like an AI chatbot behaves today. It would answer sixty percent of a question and leave the remaining forty percent visibly, deliberately open, the digital equivalent of Yama's unanswered third wish.

So here's where this series turns a corner. The solution is there, sitting in a jijnasa, a boy's refusal, and a couplet older than most of the languages spoken today. Human nature hasn't changed enough to make any of it obsolete, so there's a real case that it doesn't need reinventing, just re-applying. But knowing the shape of an old solution and actually building it into a classroom, or an AI tutor, in 2026 are two different things, and the second one has its own snags, starting with the fact that instant, total resolution is exactly what makes a chatbot feel satisfying to use in the moment, which is precisely what this fix asks it to stop doing.
