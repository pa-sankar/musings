AI is killing the curious cat

If human nature doesn't change, why would AI?

Paa Sankar September 2026 8 min read

Somewhere on Yemen's Red Sea coast this September, a fighting force whose combat footage regularly features chappals talked an entire modern military formation into abandoning a strategic port, without firing a shot at the men who left. An audio clip. A voice, cloned by AI to sound exactly like the opposing commander, calmly ordering his own troops to retreat. They retreated. The port fell. Nobody's proven the recording was fake. Nobody's proven it was real either. Curious? Read further here.

You don't need a warzone for this trick to work, incidentally. An accountant somewhere in India gets a call on an ordinary Tuesday. It's the boss, sounding exactly like the boss, urgently asking for a wire transfer before a deal closes. He sends it. The boss finds out three days later, when the deal that was never closing today comes up in a normal meeting. RBI has a name for this now, boss scam, and India's banks reported ₹48,021 crore in fraud losses in FY26 alone, a 46% jump on the year before. Curious? Read further here. Same trick as the port in Yemen. Different chappals, same feet.

Everyone's very shocked, naturally. AI is being weaponized! Except Kautilya wrote a whole chapter on exactly this move two thousand three hundred years ago, feed the target a false command from someone he trusts, and let him defeat himself before you've spent a rupee. The Houthis and whoever's running the boss-scam calls didn't invent anything. They just found a cheaper jugaad.

AI hasn't made deception possible. Humans managed that fine on their own for millennia. What it's done is made every old trick radically cheaper to run.

That's worth sitting with, because the same cheapening is happening to something much quieter, and it's happening in your kid's bedroom, not a warzone.

The trick that's cheaper now

Here's the trick. A child, or anyone, learns something specific and useful only in a narrow window: the moment they notice they don't know something and haven't yet been told. Psychologists call this an information gap, and the theory behind it, from Carnegie Mellon's George Loewenstein, is almost embarrassingly simple. You feel a gap between what you know and what you want to know, that gap produces something close to actual discomfort, and the discomfort is what makes you go find the missing piece. Curious? Read further here. Curiosity isn't a personality trait some kids have and others don't. It's a felt state with a very specific, narrow trigger window.

An AI chatbot, asked a question, closes that window in about four seconds. Complete, fluent, correct answer, no residue, no discomfort, nothing left to chase. Which is the single most efficient way ever invented to prevent the thing that was supposed to happen next.

There's a second, darker cost sitting right next to that one, and it isn't really about curiosity at all, it's about trust. We spent decades learning, the hard way, to be a little suspicious of anyone claiming certainty about something we couldn't check ourselves. Somewhere in the last two years, we forgot to point that same suspicion at a machine that has never once, in its entire existence, sounded unsure of itself, including the times it's making the whole thing up.

That's not a hypothetical. This spring, an analyst at US Special Operations Command Pacific asked a chatbot to help assess a Chinese ship's cargo manifest. The chatbot confidently concluded the vessel was carrying components for a nuclear weapons program. It was fabricating the entire thing. The finding still moved through military channels fast enough that armed personnel began preparing to board the ship and aircraft were already in the air, before someone finally stopped to check. Curious? Read further here. Nobody moved this close to a confrontation with a nuclear power because a colleague swore something was true. It happened because a chatbot said so, in the same flat, confident tone it uses to summarise a shopping list, and confidence got quietly mistaken for confirmation.

That's the actual argument for slowing down, and it has nothing to do with slowing down what AI can build. It means slowing down how fast we hand it our trust, treating a fluent, certain-sounding answer with exactly the suspicion we'd give an overconfident stranger, until it's been checked against something that isn't the chatbot itself. We already know how to do this. We just haven't gotten around to doing it here yet.

This isn't speculation on the learning side either. A 2025 study of 666 people found a significant negative relationship between how often someone uses AI and how well they think critically, with something researchers call cognitive offloading sitting in the middle as the actual mechanism. Curious? Read further here. There's an even sharper term for the worst version of it: metacognitive laziness, where a student doesn't just skip the thinking, they lose the ability to notice that they've skipped it. The output still looks fine. The AI did the work. What's missing is invisible to the person it's missing from, which is exactly what makes it dangerous, an illusion of competence with no alarm bell attached.

None of this is new either, by the way. Long before ChatGPT, researchers found that people who know a fact is searchable stop bothering to remember the fact itself, they remember where to look it up instead. Google did this quietly, over a decade. AI is doing it loudly, in about eighteen months, to reading comprehension, arithmetic, and now the actual writing of assignments.

What else did you expect

Here's the part that deserves a bit less panic and a bit more shrugging. Of course kids are using AI to skip the hard part of learning. Of course accountants are getting phished by a voice that sounds exactly like their boss. Cutting the expensive, effortful path to a goal is not a personality flaw unique to Gen Z or a moral collapse unique to 2026, it's what humans, and apparently every optimisation process we've ever built, do by default whenever the cost of the shortcut drops far enough. The mosquito coil was always worth something, we just never had a cheap enough way to make everyone pay for it. Learning was always work, we just never had a cheap enough way to make everyone skip it.

India's education system is going to feel this one hardest, because it has spent decades perfecting exactly the kind of assessment AI eats for breakfast: a fixed question, a fixed correct answer, scored the moment it lands on the page. Every restriction anyone tries next, banning phones, watermarking question papers, more proctoring software, is going to hit the same wall social media hit years ago. A bored fifteen-year-old faking an age on Instagram or routing round a school firewall with a free VPN isn't a criminal mastermind, it's just Tuesday. Restricting access to something free, instant, and quietly everywhere doesn't remove it, it just adds one extra step a determined teenager will happily learn to skip. The fix was never going to be a better lock on the door. It has to be redesigning what the assignment or the exam is actually checking, so that AI sitting right there in the room stops being a threat, because the point stops being the answer on the page and starts being whether the student can do something with it a chatbot's finished answer could never fake, kindling the curiosity AI keeps switching off, rather than pretending we can keep the tool out of the room.

So the honest question isn't "how do we stop this," which is a losing fight against a tool that isn't going anywhere. It's "given that the gap-closing machine is now free and everywhere, how do you keep a gap open long enough for a mind to actually feel it."

Which is where this gets interesting

That question turns out to have a very old answer, and not the one you'd expect. Two and a half thousand years before Loewenstein published his theory, a Sanskrit philosophical tradition built its most foundational texts around a single grammatical form meaning, roughly, the desire to know, as distinct from knowing itself, on the understanding that the desire had to come first or the whole inquiry that followed was empty. Curious? Read further here. There's a story in the Upanishads about a boy who was offered every possible bribe to stop asking a question, wealth, cattle, kingdoms, and refused every one of them because he'd rather keep the question than take the comfortable answer.

We'll get to both of those properly next time, because they're not just decoration for an argument that already works without them. They may be the actual blueprint for the fix. For now, the honest starting point is this: the gap AI keeps closing was never really about information. It was always about how long you can bear to not have it yet, and it turns out humans have been trying to answer that question, carefully, in writing, for a very long time.
