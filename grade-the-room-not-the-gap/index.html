Grade the room, not the gap you planted

If human nature doesn't change, why would AI?

Paa Sankar September 2026 6 min read

Last piece ended without a fix, and that's worth being honest about before going any further. Obfuscated arguments. Steganographic collusion. Neither one has a real solution yet. Nothing in this piece closes that gap either. What this piece does instead is step back from the question that's been driving the last two, how do you catch an AI faking understanding, and ask a different one. This series was never really about catching AI. It was about a human's curiosity, and whether it's actually happening.

That split isn't a flaw in the method. It's the method's own proof of itself. Some of what this series borrowed from old sources closed the gap clean. A felt information gap really does explain curiosity. Jijnasa really did name it first, two thousand years early. Some of it doesn't close at all. Obfuscated arguments specifically. Not with anything borrowed from two millennia back, not with anything built in the last two years either. Human nature hasn't changed enough to make either outcome surprising. It was never going to hand over one universal fix that works the same way on every problem, in every century, forever. It hands over patterns that work until they don't, and the honest job is telling which is which, not pretending every borrowed pattern closes the gap it's aimed at.

There's an old, blunt way of putting the actual boundary, and it comes in two matched halves. You can lead a horse to water, but you cannot make it drink. In one context, Gandhi said:

You can wake a man only if he's really asleep. No effort works on someone who's merely pretending to sleep.

Gandhi put the sharper half of the same point in his own autobiography, drawing on a saying that was already old by the time he reached for it.

Every fix this series has offered, the gap, the well, the foundry, the naive listener, assumes a genuinely sleeping curiosity somewhere underneath, waiting to be woken. None of it works on someone who was never actually asleep, only performing it well enough to be left alone. That's not a gap in the method. It's the actual edge of what any method, borrowed or built, old or new, was ever going to reach.

Two pieces back, this series already worked out half of the real answer. Teaching was never about transferring knowledge. It's about kindling the curiosity to go get it. Don't resolve a question fully. Leave a gap. A felt, unresolved gap is what curiosity actually runs on, whether the mind doing the learning is nine years old or ninety. The other half is what this piece is for. Once you've left the gap, how do you know the curiosity it was supposed to trigger actually happened?

Say you built a teaching tool that deliberately leaves a gap in what it presents. No full answer. Something left dangling on purpose, exactly the design this whole series has been building toward. The obvious next move is to watch whether the learner spots the gap and asks about it. That feels like proof the design worked.

It's actually the weaker signal, and once you see why, it reframes everything else this series has covered.

The trap in watching for your own gap

If you always grade success as "did they ask about the specific thing I left out," you've built something that can be gamed, exactly like every other fix in this series turned out to be gameable. Leave the gap in a predictable place often enough, and a learner learns the pattern instead of the material. There's always a blank here, they figure out. I'm meant to ask about it. That's compliance wearing curiosity's clothes. From the outside, on your metric, it looks identical to the real thing.

The more interesting, more honest signal is the opposite of what you'd expect. A learner who asks about something entirely outside the gap you planted is doing something your design didn't manufacture. They're following a question that's genuinely theirs. Self-determination theory is one of the better-established frameworks on what actually sustains motivation. Its claim is blunt: acting from your own interest, not someone else's script, is what makes curiosity durable. Everything else is a performance for the room. Curious? Read further here.

A question that lands exactly on your plan tells you your plan worked. A question that goes somewhere else tells you something considerably more valuable. You're looking at an actual mind at work, not a well-trained respondent following your breadcrumbs.

That "performance for the room" isn't abstract, either, it's close to the actual mechanism behind a large share of AI-assisted cheating in the first place. The single strongest predictor of whether a student cheats isn't their personal attitude toward honesty. It's whether they believe their peers are already doing it. One meta-analysis puts that effect among the largest known in the whole academic-dishonesty literature. It's measurably stronger, too, in classrooms with more collectivist, comparison-heavy cultures, which describes a fair amount of Indian schooling rather precisely. Curious? Read further here.

Nobody needs to be dishonest by nature for this to spread. They just need to believe everyone around them is quietly getting ahead by a shortcut they're not using, and the fear of being the one left behind does the rest.

The Gita's answer to exactly that fear predates the research by roughly two thousand years, and it happens to be the single most quoted verse in the whole text.

कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन

Karmanye vadhikaraste, ma phaleshu kadachana.
Your right is to your own action, never to its fruits.

Krishna isn't telling Arjuna to stop caring about outcomes for its own sake. He's diagnosing outcome-anxiety, measuring your own effort against everyone else's result, as the thing that corrupts right action to begin with. A student who cheats because a classmate is cheating hasn't failed at honesty so much as done exactly what the verse warns against. They've let someone else's fruit dictate their own action, instead of doing the work that was actually theirs and leaving the comparison alone.

Which is not a new discovery either

This, too, turns out to be an old idea wearing new language. The dialogic teaching structure running through the Upanishads was never a script with a fixed set of expected questions. Disciple and teacher sat close, questioning each other rather than one simply lecturing at the other. It worked because the questions were genuinely live. The same principle shows up in some of the more serious current AI research, dressed as adversarial debate. Two systems argue opposite positions in front of a judge. The idea is that an honest position can always find the flaw in a dishonest one, but only if someone's actually pushing back, not politely nodding along. Different eras, same underlying bet: unscripted challenge reveals something a scripted answer never will.

Practically, this means changing what you measure, not just what you teach. Don't grade whether they found your gap. Track whether an unprompted, genuine question got asked at all, regardless of where it lands. Vary what's left open often enough that there's no pattern to learn in the first place. That's the same reason a fixed exam paper eventually becomes worthless once enough people have seen it, while a freshly generated one doesn't. And when a student, or a curious adult, wanders off in a direction you didn't plan for, follow them there. Don't steer them back to your intended lesson. Steering them back is the pedagogical version of an over-controlled, reward-chasing environment. Research shows that kind of environment kills the intrinsic motivation you were trying to build in the first place.

Back to the port, and the chappals

This brings the series back to where it started. A fighting force in chappals didn't need better weapons to take a port. It needed one convincing voice, and a target willing to believe it without checking. An accountant didn't need to be careless either, just a call that sounded exactly right, and no habit of pausing before he acted on it. Kautilya had already written the general shape of both tricks down, twenty-three centuries before either happened. The shape of the trick was never really about the tools. It was about a target that stopped checking the moment something sounded confident enough.

Curiosity has the same shape, run in reverse. An AI chatbot doesn't need to be malicious to switch off a child's curiosity. It just needs to answer fast enough, cleanly enough, completely enough. The child never even notices the gap that was supposed to be there. Nachiketa noticed his, and refused to let Death close it cheaply. Jijnasa named the feeling two thousand years before an economist gave it a formula. None of this is a new problem asking for a clever new fix. It's the oldest problem in the species, wearing a new, considerably cheaper disguise.

None of it is the final fix either, and it shouldn't be treated as one. Jijnasa, Nachiketa, disputatio, refutation-text, process grading, grading the room instead of the gap, this is what's worked so far. It's been tried and adjusted by whoever was paying attention at the time, not installed once and left alone. The environment doesn't hold still long enough for that. AI itself is the proof. It changed the terms of this problem in about eighteen months, and it won't be the last thing that does. So the actual job was never to find the permanent answer. It's to keep doing what this series has been doing: borrow from what history already worked out, test it against whatever's changed, adjust. Dynamism isn't a flaw to be fixed here. It's the condition everything operates under.

Which changes what education is actually for. Not delivering a fixed stock of facts a student can still recite on command a decade later. Most of that evaporates anyway, regardless of how well it was taught. It's handing over the tools to keep learning, and the appetite to keep wanting to, since the learning itself doesn't really finish at graduation. Which means the honest way to check whether any of this worked isn't testing what a student currently remembers. It's whether they know how to learn something when the moment for it actually arrives, however late that turns out to be. A fact taught in a classroom and seemingly forgotten hasn't necessarily failed. It can resurface eleven years later, unprompted, in a context nobody in that classroom could have predicted. A former student suddenly needs exactly that tool, for a goal they didn't even have yet when they first learned it. That's not a lucky coincidence. That's what the teaching was for all along. It just took its time arriving.

There's a nice irony worth closing on. The first recorded person to panic that a new information technology would ruin the human mind wasn't worried about AI, or the printing press, or the internet. It was Socrates. He warned in the Phaedrus that writing itself would breed forgetfulness, and hand people the appearance of wisdom instead of the real thing. We only know he said any of this because Plato wrote it down. The warning survived by doing the exact thing it was warning against. And humanity, on the whole, turned out fine. Memory changed shape. Nobody stopped thinking.

That's the actual case for optimism here, and it isn't a hopeful guess. It's a pattern with a very long track record behind it. Writing, the printing press, the calculator, each arrived with its own version of this same panic. Each one eventually became something people think with, rather than a replacement for thinking. AI will take its place on that same list, probably within a few decades, once the panic burns off the way it always has. We don't need a new theory of the human mind to get through this one. We just need to keep being ourselves, and keep borrowing from what history already worked out. Jijnasa, Nachiketa, and a kural older than most living languages were never tied to their moment. They were tied to something that hasn't moved in thousands of years. Anchor to that, and there's a decent chance any of this would still hold up a thousand years from now, for exactly the same reason it still holds up today.

A quick word before the last piece. Thank you, genuinely, for staying the course through all five of these. If you came looking for a tidy, ready-made fix to what AI is doing to how people learn, this probably disappointed you a little, and that's fair, it was never really trying to hand you one. What it was actually trying to say is smaller than that, and I think more useful. Human nature hasn't moved much in a couple of thousand years. Once you notice that, old literature stops reading like homework and starts reading like it's still talking about you, right up until the exact same pattern turns up again in this week's AI research. That's the whole trick this series has been running. Nothing cleverer than that.

Optimism is easy to state and harder to operationalise, though. Believing humans will adapt is one thing. Actually knowing how a mind adapts, what learning is doing mechanically when it works, is another, and it's the one piece of this series that's stayed implicit so far.
