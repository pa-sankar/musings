If human nature doesn't change, why would AI?

AI hasn't introduced a new kind of problem to how humans learn, deceive, and trust each other. It's made very old patterns radically cheaper to run. This five-part series pairs a current AI example with a much older parallel, mostly Indian, making the same point, then draws out a practical implication for how people, especially in Indian education, should respond.

  1. I

    AI is killing the curious cat

    AI hasn't invented deception or shortcut-taking. It's just made every old trick cheaper to run, including the one that's quietly costing your kid their curiosity.

  2. II

    A solution from the past, rediscovered

    Long before an economist named the mechanism behind curiosity, a Sanskrit grammatical form and a boy who refused Death's bribes had already worked it out.

  3. III

    Show your work, prove you meant it

    If AI can write the answer, the answer stops proving anything. Grading the method instead of the result turns out to be an old, and surprisingly strong, fix.

  4. IV

    The peer who's too polite, and the liar who's worse

    Explaining your reasoning is a good test, right up until the person checking it doesn't really check, or the checking itself gets gamed.

  5. V

    Grade the room, not the gap you planted Coming soon

    The better signal isn't whether a learner finds a planted gap, it's whether they ask anything genuine at all.
