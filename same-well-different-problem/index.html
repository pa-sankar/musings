Same well, different problem

If human nature doesn't change, why would AI?

Paa Sankar October 2026 7 min read

Something worth admitting before this goes any further. This series started as a five-piece plan, agreed and outlined before a word of the first draft existed. This isn't part six. Call it a bonus instead, one that only exists for whoever actually stuck through all five. It showed up the same way anything worth finding does. Thinking about the other five for long enough, and testing them against actual lived experience rather than just the research sitting behind them, surfaced one more thread the original five hadn't accounted for. It took writing all the way through them to notice the gap was even there.

Which turns out to be exactly the point this piece needs to make, so leaving it out would have been a little dishonest. Knowing the tools that already exist, and staying optimistic that they'll see you through, isn't quite enough on its own. Sometimes the pattern you actually need hasn't been dug yet. The honest advice at that point isn't go draw from the well. It's go to the foundry and ask. New tools get made somewhere, by someone curious enough to go looking for where that somewhere is, and that's a different, earlier step than the one the rest of this piece is about.

Believing humans will get through this is one thing. Knowing what actually has to happen inside a mind for that to be true is another, and it's the piece this series has left unsaid until now. Worth closing on, because it turns out to be one mechanism, not many, and it's been quietly running under everything covered so far.

The transfer is the whole trick

Educational researchers draw a distinction between near transfer, applying something you've learned to a situation that looks like the one you learned it in, and far transfer, applying it somewhere that looks nothing like it on the surface but shares the same underlying shape. Near transfer is common and mostly automatic. Far transfer is rare, and it doesn't happen just because someone knows a fact. It requires spotting that two different-looking problems are, underneath, the same problem. Cognitive scientists call this analogical reasoning, and the finding that keeps replicating is that students consistently fail to make this leap even when the connection seems obvious to an expert looking on.

That failure to leap is the real dividing line this whole series has been circling, more than curiosity, more than explanation. A fact sitting in memory, unconnected to anywhere it could be used, isn't yet knowledge in any useful sense. It becomes knowledge at the exact moment it gets recognised as relevant to something it wasn't originally attached to.

An old, formal name for this exact move

Classical Indian logic had a category for it. Nyaya philosophy lists four valid ways of acquiring knowledge: perception, inference, verbal testimony, and upamana. Upamana means comparison by analogy, knowledge gained specifically by recognising that something new shares its structure with something already known. The tradition's standard example of inference makes the transfer an explicit, separate step rather than something left implicit. Someone sees smoke on a distant hill and concludes there's fire there, not because they can see the fire, but because they already know, from a kitchen, that smoke and fire travel together. The formal syllogism even names the moment of transfer on its own line, upanaya, the application. The known pattern from the kitchen gets carried over and laid on top of the unfamiliar hill. That's over two thousand years old, and it's a more precise breakdown of the mechanism than most modern accounts bother with.

Which turns out to be exactly where current AI research is stuck

This isn't just an old idea getting revived for flavour, it's live, current AI research too. François Chollet's argument for what intelligence actually is, the basis for the ARC-AGI benchmark, is that having a skill isn't intelligence. Rapidly acquiring a new skill for a problem you weren't trained on is. And the empirical result, from research published earlier this year, is striking. Humans hold close to perfect accuracy across successive, harder versions of that benchmark. Every AI approach tested drops sharply each time the puzzles stop resembling anything common in typical training data. One line from that research is worth keeping: current AI reasoning remains knowledge-bound. Which is the technical way of saying what this series has been arguing in plainer language the whole way through, pattern recognition was never the hard part. Carrying the pattern somewhere it hasn't been before is. Curious? Read further here.

Look, see, and observe are three different verbs

Something practical follows from that finding, and it's worth being blunt about it. AI is already very good at exactly the thing school spent a century optimising for, compiling, synthesising, connecting one person's work to another's. Voice interfaces are closing the last gap fast too. It barely matters anymore whether the instruction arrives typed or spoken. What's left for a human to actually do is the part AI is still measurably bad at, putting something together nobody has put together quite that way before. That isn't solved by knowing more facts. It's solved by relating to a fact differently than most reading currently teaches anyone to, using a brain instead of a pair of fingers on a keyboard.

English already has the vocabulary for this, it's just rarely used with any precision. To look at something is to register that it's there, nothing more. Sherlock Holmes made the next step famous, telling Watson flatly that he sees but does not observe, and the line still lands because most people, most of the time, genuinely do stop there. India has old words for the same climb, and they're worth using precisely rather than loosely. Darśana, the term the six classical schools of Indian philosophy are named for, isn't a passive glance. It's meaningful, perceptual seeing, recognising what a thing actually is. Prekṣā goes further still, built from the root īkṣ, to see, with the intensifying prefix pra added to it, to perceive carefully and profoundly. It's the word a real, still-practiced Jain meditation tradition uses for its own core discipline, traced back to a line in the Dasavaikalika Sutra.

Sampikkhae appagamappaenam.

See the self through the self.

Look is contact. Darśana is recognition. Prekṣā is the noticing of what a thing implies, what it connects to, what would follow if it turned out to be true somewhere else too.

Reading a fact is looking. Understanding what it means is seeing. Neither one, by itself, is the vijnana this piece already spent a section on. Observing, in the sense that actually matters here, is what turns a fact into something available for original thought later. It gets wired to enough else that a mind can reach for it in a context nobody planned, the same mechanism as the well from a few pieces back. A student who has only ever looked and seen has plenty of jnana and nothing to build with. A student who has observed has raw material for a thought nobody else has had yet. That's the one thing a machine that's very good at recombining everyone else's thoughts still can't reliably do on its own.

The two ways this goes wrong, and what fixes each one

The failure mode where a pattern gets learned but never carried anywhere got an explicit name in 1929, from Alfred North Whitehead. He called it inert knowledge, and his verdict on it was harsher than most teachers would risk saying out loud. Education built on inert knowledge isn't just wasted effort, it's actively harmful, because it leaves someone believing they know something they can't actually use. Curious? Read further here.

The Bhagavad Gita draws the identical line, centuries earlier, and names both sides of it. Jnana, knowledge held theoretically, against vijnana, knowledge that's been realised, made functional, lived. One commentary on it puts the gap in almost domestic terms. Knowing that fire can be drawn from wood is jnana. Kindling the fire and feeling its heat on a cold night is vijnana. A person can hold a flawless jnana and still fail every moment that actually calls for it, by not catching the pattern.

The fix for that has a name too, and it's sitting a few couplets along in the same Thirukkural chapter this series has already leaned on twice.

தொட்டனைத் தூறும் மணற்கேணி மாந்தர்க்குக் கற்றனைத் தூறும் அறிவு.

Water flows from a well dug in sand in proportion to how deep it's dug, and knowledge flows from a person in proportion to how much they learn.

The detail worth noticing is what a sand well actually does under use. It doesn't run dry from being drawn on. It flows more freely. Knowledge that gets revisited, applied, carried somewhere new and brought back again, behaves the same way. That lines up precisely with what modern research on memory calls the testing effect. Recalling something and putting it to work strengthens it far more than simply reading it over again ever does. Inert knowledge is the well nobody goes back to.

Ekalavya is the well worked to its limit. Refused as a student by Dronacharya, the archery teacher to the Kuru princes, he went into the forest alone. He built a clay image of the guru who'd turned him away, and practiced in front of it, year after year, until he outstripped every prince who'd actually been taught. Nobody handed him a single new fact along the way. He just kept returning to the same well, digging the same hole deeper, long after anyone would have called it enough, and the water kept coming.

Which is the actual, practical close to this whole series

By knowing how learning happens, and it happens through this one repeated move, you don't need a new theory every time a new problem shows up. Recognising that a new problem carries the shape of an old one, and carrying the old answer across to fit it, is the whole trick. You need practice at making that specific leap, and enough returned-to, well-worn patterns lying around to leap from.

Which, looked at honestly, is exactly what this series has spent six pieces doing to AI itself. An AI voice clone fooling a fighting force in Yemen wasn't a new problem, it was Kautilya's oldest trick, given a cheaper vendor. A chatbot closing a curiosity gap before a child feels it wasn't a new problem either, it was Nachiketa's bribe, minus the courtesy of Yama actually offering one. Grading a memorised answer instead of a genuine explanation, a naive listener who's really just being polite, a well that goes dry from neglect, none of these needed an original idea. They needed the oldest available match, recognised, and carried across the gap into 2026. Five pieces of the well, worked once more.

This piece is the other half. When the five-piece well ran dry partway through writing it, the fix wasn't to force a sixth bucket out of the same hole. It was to go looking for where a new one gets made. That's a foundry, not a well, and the only way to find a foundry is to stay curious enough to go ask. Jijnasa again, right at the very end, exactly where it started.

Learning from history has never been more fun.

One last equation, then. AI, applied without NI, natural intelligence, pulling its own weight alongside it, doesn't add up to more intelligence. It adds up to ZI. Zero intelligence, dressed well enough to pass for a while. The one thing on this entire planet built exactly like nobody else is the brain sitting behind your own eyes. Use it. That's the whole advice.
